The best family trip is not the longest or the busiest; it is the one that fits your family. A program that thrills teenagers exhausts toddlers, and a pace that suits grandparents bores a 12-year-old. Use these six steps to choose the right tour program before you book, whether with Arcadia or any other company.

1. Define your family's pace

Count who is travelling. With children under 6 or elderly relatives: one main activity per day, a rest after lunch, short drives (under two hours) and no more than one hotel change every three nights. With children aged 7–12: one big activity plus one light one per day, and theme parks or nature days every second day. With teenagers: full days are fine, and they usually prefer adventure (canyons, cable cars, skiing) over museums. Tell the company the ages; a good program is built around them.

2. Pick the right duration

  • 5–6 days: one city plus day trips (Almaty, Warsaw, Tashkent and Samarkand, or Moscow).
  • 7–8 days: two cities with a comfortable pace: Almaty and Astana, Warsaw and Krakow, Moscow and St. Petersburg, Samarkand and Bukhara.
  • 10 days or more: three cities or two countries (Kazakhstan with Uzbekistan, Beijing with Xi'an and Shanghai).

Avoid cramming: three cities in seven days means three hotel changes and two travel days, which is a third of the trip spent packing.

3. Hotels and location

Location beats star rating for families. A central 4-star within walking distance of restaurants and a park saves hours of traffic every day compared with a 5-star in the suburbs. Ask for the hotel name and check: connecting rooms or a family suite, a pool (essential in Uzbekistan in summer), breakfast included, and halal restaurants nearby. In Almaty choose the centre or near the mountains; in Krakow the Old Town; in Samarkand and Bukhara the historic quarter; in Moscow near a central metro station.

4. Activities everyone enjoys

Mix the four types every day or two: a landmark (Registan, Red Square, Wawel), a nature or outdoor activity (Big Almaty Lake, Peterhof gardens, Zakopane cable car), something interactive for children (science centres, aqua parks, Energylandia, Universal Beijing) and free time for shopping or a pool. Ask how much walking each day involves and whether strollers can be used (the Moscow and St. Petersburg metros are hard with strollers; most old towns are fine).

5. Set a clear budget

Decide the total you want to spend excluding flights, and tell the company; it is far easier to design a good program for 3,000 USD than to cut a 5,000 USD program afterwards. Typical ranges for a family of four for a week are 2,200–3,900 USD in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Poland and 3,000–4,500 USD in Russia; see how much a family trip costs. Compare quotes by inclusions, not headline prices.

6. Choose the season for the destination

Summer for Kazakhstan, Poland and Russia; spring and autumn for Uzbekistan and China; winter for snow in Almaty or Zakopane. The month changes the whole experience, so check the best time for family travel before fixing the destination, and the best destinations for Arab families if you are still deciding.

Checklist before you confirm

  • A written day-by-day program with named hotels and a fixed price.
  • Inclusions and exclusions listed (tickets, transfers, guide days, meals).
  • Children's ages reflected in the activities and the hotel rooms.
  • A local office and a WhatsApp contact during the trip.
  • Clear payment and cancellation terms.

Frequently asked questions

Can a program suit both toddlers and teenagers?
Yes: a base-city program with a pool hotel, where teenagers take an adventure day with the guide while the parents and toddlers have a slow day.

How much free time should a program have?
About a third: one free afternoon every two days and one fully free day per week.

Should we book activities ourselves to save money?
Rarely worth it; the company buys tickets at local prices and avoids sold-out days.

What if we want to change the program after booking?
Most changes are possible before departure and many on the spot; ask about the policy in advance.
