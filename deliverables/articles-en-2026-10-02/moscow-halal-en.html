More than two million Muslims live in Moscow, so finding halal food there is easier than many Gulf travellers expect: Uzbek, Tatar, Caucasian, Turkish and Arabic restaurants across the city, restaurants near the Cathedral Mosque, and food courts in the big malls with clearly marked halal counters. Unlike Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, though, you cannot assume that every restaurant in Moscow is halal. Traditional Russian cuisine uses a lot of pork, and many restaurants serve alcohol. This guide explains where a family can eat with confidence, how to check, which dishes are worth trying, prices in roubles, and where to pray.

How do you recognise a halal restaurant in Moscow?

  • Look for the word "Халяль" on the storefront, on the menu, or on the restaurant's page in Yandex Maps, the app that works in Russia in place of Google Maps.
  • Uzbek, Tatar, Dagestani, Azerbaijani, Turkish and Arabic restaurants: their meat is usually halal; ask to confirm.
  • Russian, European and Asian restaurants (sushi, Chinese): assume nothing. Pork ("свинина") is common in main dishes, soups and salads.
  • The question in Russian is "Это халяль?". On an Arcadia program, your Arabic-speaking guide takes care of this at every meal.

Best types of halal restaurants for Muslim families

1. Uzbek chains (the easiest choice in every district)

Uryuk (Урюк) is one of the best-known halal chains in Moscow, with dozens of branches serving plov, shashlik, manty, lagman and tandoor bread at reasonable prices, in an oriental setting that suits families. Chaihona No. 1 (Чайхона №1) is the most widespread chain in the city; it states that its meat is halal and serves Uzbek and Middle Eastern cuisine, though note that some branches serve alcohol. Both are found in malls, on the main streets and near the major sights.

2. Caucasian restaurants (shashlik and kebab)

Dagestani, Azerbaijani and Chechen restaurants serve charcoal-grilled shashlik, khinkal, kebab and fresh bread. Most are halal and serve tea rather than alcohol. They are common around the large metro stations and in the Prospekt Mira district near the Cathedral Mosque.

3. Tatar restaurants (the cuisine of Russia's native Muslims)

Tatar cuisine is halal by tradition: sweet chak-chak, echpochmak (triangular pastries filled with meat and potato) and Tatar soup. You will find these restaurants in the Zamoskvorechye district, near the Historical Mosque on Bolshaya Tatarskaya Street, a neighbourhood close to the Kremlin.

4. Turkish and Arabic restaurants

Moscow has dozens of Turkish restaurants (kebab, pide, lahmacun) and Lebanese, Syrian and Egyptian restaurants serving grills, shawarma, kabsa, mandi and hummus, most of them in the city centre and around Arbat and Tverskaya. Names change from year to year; the Arcadia team will point you to the ones that are open near your hotel.

5. Mall food courts

Afimall in Moscow City, Aviapark, the largest mall in Europe, and Evropeisky near Kievskaya station have marked halal counters (shawarma, plov, kebab) and prayer rooms or quiet rooms. They are the easiest option with children.

6. Fast food

The large Russian fast-food chains are generally not halal, apart from a limited number of branches with a halal certificate. The alternative is the halal shawarma kiosks found everywhere (the sign reads "шаурма"), provided they display a halal sign, and the small halal burger chains in the malls.

Dishes worth trying

  • Uzbek plov, shashlik, manty and lagman in Uzbek restaurants.
  • Dagestani khinkal and Caucasian kebab with lavash bread.
  • Echpochmak and chak-chak from the Tatar kitchen.
  • Russian pelmeni and borscht, if the restaurant makes them with halal beef; ask first.
  • Sweet Russian pancakes (blini) with honey or berries are a safe choice anywhere.

How much does food cost in Moscow?

Prices are approximate for 2026 (one Saudi riyal is about 21–24 roubles):

  • Shawarma or a halal fast-food meal: 300–500 roubles (about 14–24 SAR, or $4–6).
  • Mid-range Uzbek or Caucasian restaurant: 1,200–2,000 roubles per person (about 55–95 SAR, or $15–25).
  • Upscale restaurant with a view: 3,500–6,000 roubles per person (about 160–280 SAR, or $43–75).
  • A family of four for a full day: 6,000–10,000 roubles (about 280–470 SAR, or $75–125).

Where to pray in Moscow

  • Moscow Cathedral Mosque on Prospekt Mira: one of the largest mosques in Europe, reopened after its expansion in 2015, with room for ten thousand worshippers and halal restaurants around it.
  • The Historical Mosque on Bolshaya Tatarskaya Street near Novokuznetskaya station, minutes from the Kremlin.
  • Yardyam Mosque in the north-east of the city, and prayer rooms in some malls and at the airports (Sheremetyevo and Domodedovo both have prayer rooms).
  • In large hotels you can ask reception for a prayer mat and the direction of the qibla.

Practical tips

  • Book popular restaurants for Friday and Saturday evenings, especially in summer.
  • Main dishes in Uzbek restaurants are large; one dish is enough for two children.
  • Black tea ("чай") is served everywhere, and bottled water is cheap in supermarkets.
  • During Ramadan, the restaurants near the Cathedral Mosque serve group iftar meals.
  • For allergies: "орехи" means nuts, "молоко" milk and "глютен" gluten.

Frequently asked questions

Is halal food easy to find in Moscow?
Yes. There are Uzbek, Caucasian, Tatar, Turkish and Arabic restaurants in every district, and most display the "Халяль" sign.

Are Russian fast-food chains halal?
Generally not, apart from a limited number of certified branches. Halal shawarma and the halal counters in malls are the better choice.

Are there Arabic restaurants in Moscow?
Yes: Lebanese, Syrian, Egyptian and Moroccan restaurants in the city centre, and our team will point you to the nearest.

How do I pay in restaurants?
In cash in roubles, or with a local Mir card, which we can arrange for you, because Gulf bank cards do not work.

Where can I pray Friday prayers in Moscow?
At the Cathedral Mosque on Prospekt Mira. Go early, because attendance is very high.
